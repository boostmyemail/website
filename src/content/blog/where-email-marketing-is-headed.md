Email marketing has two paths to take in 2027. I'm not particularly excited about one of them, and I think the odds are we end up building the unfavorable one.

## Is email performance actually getting worse?

Darrell Alfonso recently put out a poll asking people where they thought email was headed. Essentially, he was asking whether they'd seen better performance lately, the same, or worse.

![Darrell Alfonso's LinkedIn poll, "Is email marketing getting harder, or are you seeing better results?" Results from 189 votes: 9% seeing stronger results, 31% about the same as before, 39% performance is slipping, 20% results have dropped sharply.](/assets/darrell-alfonso-email-poll.jpg)

It's not a surprise, but 59% of email marketers say their performance is in decline. And I don't mean to speak for many, but for those that said it's the same as before... I would wager theirs is also slipping.

My own campaigns tell a split story. There's a very specific subset that has seen a bit of a lift in engagement (I'll get to these later). The majority, though, are fighting the same thing everybody else is fighting: a general decline. These are programs doing all the normal, good things. Curating the list, working the copy, and so on. They're just kind of dropping anyway.

My split is exactly what makes me think I can see where things are headed. But before getting into the two possible outcomes, I want to talk about why there's so much fatigue in email in the first place.

## Why everyone's inbox feels so tired

Email is unique. Technically there's plenty of filtering going on, but the way people experience it feels like every message lands in the inbox. It isn't curated for them the way a social media feed is. It's a stream of information. So people feel overwhelmed by how much is coming at them.

The sheer volume is the most critical issue in email right now. There's. so. much. We just don't have the time or energy to give every message real focus. So by nature, every message becomes less relevant, because we don't have the capacity to consume it. That's the fatigue everybody's feeling.

What that means for each individual message is that the bar for relevance is far higher than it used to be. And I'm defining relevance as a message's ability to actually meet customers where they are. That goes way beyond first-name personalization.

## Inbox providers are pulling people out of the inbox

All that volume is stressing the system. And the system, meaning the inbox providers, is trying to deal with it. They're looking for ways to take some of that burden off the people they serve.

You can see it in a lot of Gmail's recent releases. A while back they rolled out a managed subscriptions view. Instead of the preference center living in each brand's email service provider, you can manage all of your subscriptions in one place inside Gmail. It shows you how many emails you've received from each brand over a recent stretch of time. Then you pick the ones you want to unsubscribe from, or use list-unsubscribe to get out.

Gmail is also building an AI inbox, plus a live mode where you can talk to an agent, ask it what you need, and it finds the information for you.

So Gmail is literally shipping features that pull people away from your message. The goal is to lighten what users have to look at and surface only what's relevant. That's good for the people reading email. It creates a lot of problems for you and me.

## The good world, part one: meet contacts where they actually are

So where is email marketing headed? Let's start with the positive side, because I do think a good version of this future exists. For it to happen, two things have to be true.

The first is on the brand side, that's you and me. Personalization has to reach a level we've never gotten to before. I've posted about this principle quite a bit, but let me illustrate with an example.

One of my clients is Glide. It started as a no-code app builder and is now expanding into full operating systems and workflows for businesses, because AI makes that pretty easily possible. Say I'm a business owner or an operator, and I need a custom internal app. I come to Glide, and the very first thing I do is type in my problem, or what I want to build.

As the email guy, I get all of that information. I know exactly what each user wants out of their experience with Glide.

Traditionally, personalization meant looking for keywords, or classifying users by the company they work at, sorting them into segments, and personalizing content for each segment. Not anymore. I now use what people told me they're thinking. When someone tells me the specific app they want to build, our onboarding emails reference *what they wrote* and show how Glide meets them at that exact problem.

I've been running this experiment for about a year. The engagement on our first campaign increased by 3x. Our standard welcome email had a 1.8% click-through rate. The personalized version jumped to north of 5%.

Everything in that email changed: the subject line, the header, all of it, written to speak to what that contact told me. That relevance really clicked for users. The brand, as a system, finally understood where they were and where they wanted to go. And it's completely fine to get that personal and use a system to do it, because it's a brand.

This approach has real requirements, though.

Contacts have to share enough information to build on. An e-commerce brand that gets an email address from a discount pop-up on its homepage won't have the same ability to understand what customers want. It can't craft emails at the level of individual thinking the way Glide's onboarding can.

It also takes a lot of data infrastructure. There's really creative work in building these campaigns and figuring out the technology and workflow tools to make them happen. If you're a PLG company with high inbound volume, the workflow costs can be high.

## The good world, requirement two: people curate their own inbox

The second thing is both inbox providers and the people using them have to curate the inbox. They have to cut out the fat.

And who does the cutting matters a lot.

If the inbox providers do it, email marketers lose visibility. We'll keep sending messages, but we won't know whether they hit the inbox or whether they were hidden from our contacts.

If users do it, that's better. Unsubscribes tell the email marketer that they can't mail that person anymore. Sure, the list shrinks. But in theory it gets really tight, because people are only letting in the brands they care about.

That's the good world.

## The more likely world: everybody plays the volume game

I think the negative world has a higher chance of happening, and it's pretty simple. Everybody plays the volume game.

People don't curate their inbox, because it's work. Agents and filters sort mail into tabs and block certain senders, so you don't have to worry about it. And if you can just ask your AI inbox for the most important things, email marketers have absolutely no control over visibility. A system decides how relevant your message is.

In that world, I'm honestly not sure how much nailing the personalization lane even matters. The creative gets filtered out. The imagery gets filtered out. You've got a system reading your messages, and my guess is it just wants straight information.

Email doesn't go away in that world. It'll never go away. But it becomes a more system-driven, AI-based channel. Your fancy copy has no chance.

## The choice every brand makes in the next five years

This leaves brands with a couple of choices.

**Option one:** invest in building really unique, differentiated email content that most other brands are unwilling to do.

**Option two:** cut costs and basically turn email marketing over to a machine. Copy's AI copy. Segmentation's AI segmentation. It's good enough. It gets shipped, and the resources move somewhere else.

My hope is for the first one. It's a better experience for everybody. But looking at the volume going out, I'm afraid we're headed down the other road. I do see inbox providers trying to improve the experience. I don't see people curating their own inboxes the way we'd want them to. That leaves email marketers sending message after message that gets filtered by algorithms, with no idea what happens to it.

So it's changing. If you want to stay in email and really prove its value, you've got to figure out the personalized lane: understanding the individual person's mindset and how the brand should speak to them. If you don't figure that out, it's going to be really hard to set your emails apart from everything else AI is shipping.

That's where we're at. I'd genuinely like to hear where you think it's headed.
